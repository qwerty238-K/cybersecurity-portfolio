## 5. Learning Journey

My cybersecurity learning journey has shown me that understanding a concept in a lecture is different from applying it in a real or simulated environment. At the beginning, I was more comfortable with programming tasks than with analysing network traffic or explaining the behaviour of security tools. Working through practical exercises helped me become more systematic. Rather than treating an output as a final answer, I have learned to check the environment, record the command or configuration used, examine the evidence, and state what the results do and do not prove.

One topic that has strengthened my understanding is network reconnaissance with Nmap. During authorised scanning exercises, I learned that an open port alone does not establish whether a host is vulnerable. Port states, service fingerprints, target configuration, and scan options all affect interpretation. I practised organising results in a format that makes it easier to distinguish observed services from assumptions. This was useful because it encouraged me to explain the scope and limitations of a scan rather than presenting a list of ports without context.

A second important topic has been packet analysis with Wireshark. Examining TCP connections helped me connect familiar theoretical ideas, such as the three-way handshake, to individual packets. Source and destination addresses, ports, TCP flags, and sequence information provide evidence about the behaviour of a connection. I found that a packet capture becomes more meaningful when I can relate each observation to a specific question. This encouraged me to improve how I annotate screenshots and document a repeatable procedure.

One challenge I encountered in practical work was troubleshooting results that did not match my expectations. When network connectivity or a scan produced unexpected output, repeating the same command was not always helpful. A more productive approach was to verify the virtual machine network settings, confirm the target address, check connectivity, and then review the relevant tool options. I learned to make one change at a time, record the outcome, and separate environmental problems from errors in interpreting results. This is an approach I want to continue using in future investigations.

I still find professional security monitoring and SIEM investigation challenging. Reading a basic scan result is different from working with large volumes of logs, identifying meaningful indicators, and deciding which events require escalation. I need more practice connecting technical evidence to an incident timeline and explaining findings to both technical and non-technical readers.

My improvement goal is to complete at least three documented log-analysis or network-investigation exercises by 30 June 2027. For each exercise, I will keep the objective, lab scope, tools, commands or queries, screenshots, findings, and limitations. I will consider the goal achieved when I can reproduce each exercise, explain the evidence in a short written report, and identify at least one improvement for the next attempt. This portfolio will serve as a record of that progress.
